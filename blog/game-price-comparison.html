How to compare PC game prices across stores

Reviewed July 2026 · LootRadar guide

The cheapest listing on the page is not always the thing you meant to buy. PC stores sell different editions, hand out keys for different launchers, lock some offers to certain regions, and occasionally show you one number that becomes a different number at checkout. So the first job in any price comparison is boring but essential: make sure you are comparing the same product.

The checklist below takes about two minutes. It also saves you from the classic bargain-hunter achievement, the one where you save three dollars and end up owning the wrong edition of a game you now cannot refund.

Why one game has five different prices

Stores run their promotions on completely different schedules. A publisher might hand one retailer a two-week discount while everyone else sits at full price. Add membership perks, store coupons, bundle math, and regional pricing, and the spread on a single game gets wide fast.

A cheaper price is not automatically shady. What you want to know is who is selling it, what is actually in the box, which launcher the key wakes up in, and whether the offer even applies where you live.

Check which edition you are looking at

Standard, Deluxe, Complete, Definitive, Game of the Year. These are not synonyms, no matter how similar the store pages look. One includes every expansion, the next adds a hat and a soundtrack, and remasters love to share a name with the original release they replaced.

  • Match the full product title, including the release it belongs to.
  • Compare the DLC that is actually included, not the word on the label.
  • Work out whether a bundle contains the base game or quietly assumes you own it.
  • Watch for upgrade packs sitting right next to full-game listings, wearing the same art.

Sort out region and activation before you get excited

A PC game might arrive as a Steam key, a key for some other launcher, a direct download, or a DRM-free installer you can back up forever. Decide which of those you are actually willing to live with before the price starts making decisions for you.

Then read the retailer page properly: region restrictions, required accounts, supported operating systems, refund terms, and any small note about keys being delivered “within 24 hours.” A comparison page is a shortcut to that information. It is not a substitute for it, and it never gets the final say. The store does.

A percentage is context, not proof

That big number could mean a genuinely great price, a list price that was pumped up so it could be cut back down, or a game that has been “on sale” more or less continuously since launch. What the percentage cannot tell you is whether the game is good, whether this is simply its normal price now, or whether you want it.

So check the player feedback and how many people left it. If there is trustworthy price history, hold the current number up against past sales. If there is not, judge it against the game itself and what you are honestly happy to spend. And remember that waiting is free and works every time.

The stores are not interchangeable

Steam, GOG, Epic, publisher storefronts, and authorized key sellers each suit different people. Steam has the library, the community tools, and the refund policy everyone else is measured against. GOG sells DRM-free builds that are yours whatever happens to a launcher. Key sellers can undercut both on a code for someone else’s launcher. None of that shows up in the price.

Comparison tools cut down your tab count, which is genuinely useful, but every one of them is limited to the stores it covers and the data it can see. Treat any comparison page as a sample of the market, never the whole thing.

Let the retailer confirm the number

Prices and stock move between a snapshot and your checkout screen. Taxes, currency conversion, account-level discounts, and regional rules all get a vote in the final total. Before you pay, check:

  • the exact edition and what is inside it;
  • the activation platform and the region;
  • the real total after discounts and tax;
  • the refund and support terms of that specific store.
